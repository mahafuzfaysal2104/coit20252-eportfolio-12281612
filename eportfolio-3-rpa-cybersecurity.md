# Artefact 1 — Process Cybersecurity and Cyber Threats

## Summary

The *Cyber Security Insights 2025* report shows how cyber threats can affect organisational processes and information systems. Common incidents include compromised accounts and credentials, malware, and compromised networks or infrastructure. The report also highlights phishing, AI-enhanced hacking tools and increasing third-party cyber incidents as important threats faced by organisations (Audit Office of New South Wales 2025, p. 4).

## Justification

I selected this artefact because cybersecurity is not only an IT issue; it can directly affect the reliability and continuity of business processes. This connects with Week 8, where process cybersecurity focuses on protecting processes, systems and information. I found the report particularly useful because it also discusses practical controls such as security awareness training, patching, multi-factor authentication and restricting administrative privileges (Audit Office of New South Wales 2025, p. 20). These controls demonstrate how organisations can reduce cybersecurity risks while maintaining secure and resilient processes.

# Artefact 2 — Risk Management and Risk Assessment in BPM

## Summary

Tomaskova et al. (2025) demonstrate how Failure Mode and Effects Analysis (FMEA) can be combined with business process analysis to identify and prioritise risks. FMEA evaluates possible process failures using severity, occurrence and detectability and calculates a Risk Priority Number (RPN) to identify vulnerable process activities (Tomaskova et al. 2025, p. 100). BPMN is also used to visually represent the workflow and identify opportunities to improve efficiency and risk management (Tomaskova et al. 2025, p. 101).

## Justification

I chose this artefact because it connects BPM and the risk assessment process discussed in Week 9. Instead of treating risks separately from the process, the study integrates risk information into process analysis. I found the measurable results especially interesting: after sensor integration, the total RPN decreased from 652.5 to 114.5, representing an 82% reduction in overall risk (Tomaskova et al. 2025, p. 111). This shows how risk identification, analysis, treatment, and monitoring can support evidence-based business process improvement.

# Artefact 3 — Lean Six Sigma for Business Process Improvement

## Summary

Lean Six Sigma combines two process improvement approaches. Lean concentrates on removing waste and improving process flow, while Six Sigma aims to reduce defects and process variation. Combining these approaches provides organisations with a structured way to achieve continuous process improvement (Sakib, Kawsar & Bithee 2025, p. 1262). The DMAIC framework — Define, Measure, Analyse, Improve and Control — also provides a systematic approach for identifying and improving process problems (Sakib, Kawsar & Bithee 2025, p. 1261).

## Justification

I selected this artefact because it directly supports the Week 10 discussion of continuous business process improvement. I learned that process improvement is not simply about making a process faster. Organisations also need to consider waste, variation, quality and long-term performance. The study highlights the value of Lean Six Sigma across different organisational settings and explains its role in supporting operational excellence and continuous improvement (Sakib, Kawsar & Bithee 2025, pp. 1263–1264). This makes Lean Six Sigma useful when organisations need a structured and data-driven approach to improving business processes.

# Artefact 4 — Lean vs Six Sigma

## Summary

Lindfors and Wiksfors (2025) explain that Lean and Six Sigma have different but complementary process improvement purposes. Lean focuses on eliminating waste, reducing bottlenecks and improving the flow of value-adding activities (Lindfors & Wiksfors 2025, p. 1). In contrast, Six Sigma focuses on strategic problem-solving and reducing process variation through the DMAIC cycle: Define, Measure, Analyze, Improve and Control (Lindfors & Wiksfors 2025, p. 2).

## Justification

I chose this Master's thesis because it helped me clearly understand the difference between Lean and Six Sigma from a practical process-improvement perspective. Lean is particularly useful for identifying waste and creating smoother process flow, whereas Six Sigma provides a systematic method for investigating problems and reducing variation. The thesis also explains that Lean Six Sigma combines DMAIC with Lean tools so that organisations can address errors, waste and variation while improving production flow (Lindfors & Wiksfors 2025, p. 2). This directly relates to Week 10 and shows why the two approaches can complement each other rather than compete.
